Part 1: Governance & Data

Governance: Foundations for Data Science in the Public Sector

Key message: Governance underpins trustworthy public sector data science.

  • Data: Can we access, use, and trust the information?
  • Algorithm: Are models fair, explainable, and accountable?
  • Platform: Are systems secure and appropriately controlled?

Three foundations covered: - Privacy law: Individual rights and legal obligations. - Māori Data Sovereignty: Māori authority and collective rights and interests. - Data governance: Policies, roles, and processes that put these into practice.

Example: A government AI project needs lawful data access, appropriate Māori data governance, model oversight, and secure infrastructure.

Why Privacy, Ethics & Data Sovereignty Matter

  • Privacy → Protects individuals from misuse of personal information.
  • Ethics → Promotes fairness, respect, and responsible data use.
  • Māori Data Sovereignty → Recognises collective rights and Māori authority over Māori data (Kukutai et al. 2023).

Together, they support trust, accountability, and responsible Data Science.

Key message: Legal compliance alone does not guarantee ethical or culturally appropriate data use.

Examples: - Privacy: A researcher must have a lawful basis to access identifiable health records and protect them against unauthorised access. - Ethics: An AI model predicting welfare needs may reinforce existing inequalities if its training data reflects historical bias. - Māori Data Sovereignty: Research using Māori health data should consider Māori authority, governance, and collective benefit, even when individual privacy is protected.

Discussion: Can a data science project be legally compliant but still cause harm?

Transition: We begin with the legal foundation — New Zealand’s Privacy Act 2020.

Privacy Legal Foundations in Aotearoa New Zealand

  • Privacy Act 2020 → Governs the handling of personal information (New Zealand Parliament 2020).
  • Replaced the Privacy Act 1993, strengthening protections for the digital age.
  • Applies broadly to public and private sector agencies, subject to exceptions.
  • Regulated by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner.
  • Establishes 13 Information Privacy Principles (IPPs), with an additional IPP 3A effective from 1 May 2026.

Key message: The Privacy Act establishes the legal foundation for handling personal information in New Zealand.

Examples: - Government: An agency collecting income or employment records must comply with applicable privacy requirements. - Research: A university using identifiable patient data must consider lawful collection, use, disclosure, and security. - AI: Training a model using personal information does not remove privacy obligations.

Important distinction: The Act primarily protects information about identifiable individuals. Fully anonymised information generally falls outside its scope, although re-identification risks remain relevant.

2026 update: IPP 3A introduces notification requirements for indirect collection, subject to exceptions.

Transition: The 13 IPPs translate these legal obligations into practical rules for handling personal information.

The 13 IPPs (1–7)

Theme # Principle Key Idea
Collection 1 Purpose of collection Collect information only for a lawful and necessary purpose.
2 Source of information Collect directly from the individual where possible.
3 Collection from individual Must inform people about what, why, and how data is used.
1 May 2026 3a* Indirect Collection Must inform people about what, why, and how data is used, if collect from someone other than the person themselves.
4 Manner of collection Collect fairly and lawfully, respecting dignity and context.
Storage & Security 5 Storage and security Protect information from loss, misuse, or unauthorised access.

Key message: IPPs 1–5 regulate how personal information is collected and protected.

  • IPPs 1–4: Collect necessary information, explain its purpose, and use fair collection methods.
  • IPP 3A (2026): Indirect collection generally requires notification, subject to exceptions.
  • IPP 5: Apply reasonable security safeguards.

Examples: - Surveys: A diabetes study collects age, ethnicity, income, and health history. Researchers should justify why each variable is necessary and inform participants how their data will be used. - Social media scraping: Researchers collect public Facebook posts to analyse mental health attitudes. Public availability does not automatically permit unrestricted collection or reuse; fairness, notification, and applicable exceptions must be considered. - Health records: A university stores identifiable patient records for AI training. Encryption, role-based access, audit logs, and secure research environments help prevent unauthorised access.

Discussion: Does individual consent necessarily mean collecting data from Māori communities is culturally appropriate?

The 13 IPPs (8–13)

Theme # Principle Key Idea
Access & Correction 6 Access People have a right to see their personal information.
7 Correction People can correct or update their information.
Use & Accuracy 8 Accuracy Ensure data is up to date and correct before use.
9 Retention Don’t keep information longer than necessary.
10 Use limitation Use only for the original purpose collected.
Disclosure 11 Disclosure Share only when authorised or expected.
Cross-border & Identifiers 12 Cross-border disclosure Safeguard information sent overseas (ensure similar protections).
13 Unique identifiers Use unique IDs only when necessary and non-misleading.

Source: Office of the Privacy Commissioner — NZ Privacy Principles

Key message: IPPs 6–13 govern individual rights and the subsequent use, retention, and disclosure of information.

  • IPPs 6–7: Individuals can request access to and correction of their information.
  • IPPs 8–9: Take reasonable steps to ensure accuracy and avoid unnecessary retention.
  • IPPs 10–11: Secondary use and disclosure require a permitted basis.
  • IPP 12: Overseas disclosure requires appropriate safeguards.
  • IPP 13: Restricts unique identifiers, with specific research provisions.

Examples: - AI prediction (IPP 8): A hospital predicts readmission risk using outdated diagnoses and incomplete clinical records. Poor data quality can produce inaccurate predictions and potentially disadvantage certain groups. - IDI data linkage (IPPs 10 & 13): Researchers link health, income, and education records to investigate diabetes risk. The research purpose, legal permissions, identifier handling, and disclosure controls must be assessed. - Cloud storage (IPPs 5 & 12): A university uses an overseas cloud provider to process patient data. Researchers must assess security, contractual safeguards, and whether the arrangement constitutes overseas disclosure.

Transition: The Privacy Act primarily protects individuals, but population-level analysis can affect entire communities. Who should have authority over data about those communities?

  • They are largely individual-centric — focused on individual rights, not collective data control.
  • This raises key questions for Māori contexts: Can privacy laws evolve to recognise collective data sovereignty? (Watts and Kukutai 2025)

Māori Data Sovereignty — What It Means

  • Māori Data = Data about Māori people, communities, and resources.
  • Data generated by Māori
  • Data about Māori collected by others
  • Data about Māori land, language, culture, and environment
  • Māori Data Sovereignty = Māori rights to exercise authority, control, and self-determination over Māori data.

Guiding Principles — Te Mana Raraunga

  • Rangatiratanga → Authority and self-determination
  • Whakapapa → Relationships and context
  • Whanaungatanga → Obligations and accountability
  • Kotahitanga → Collective benefit
  • Manaakitanga → Respect and reciprocity
  • Kaitiakitanga → Guardianship and stewardship

Key message: Protecting individual privacy does not necessarily address collective rights, governance, or community impacts.

Example — Māori diabetes risk prediction:

Researchers develop an AI model using linked health and socioeconomic records. The model identifies higher predicted diabetes risk among Māori communities.

Even if individual records are protected and published findings are aggregated: - Authority: Were Māori involved in deciding the research purpose and how their data would be used? - Representation: Could the findings reinforce stereotypes if socioeconomic and historical contexts are ignored? - Benefit: Will the results improve Māori health services, or primarily benefit researchers?

Six principles — Te Mana Raraunga: - Rangatiratanga: Māori authority over data decisions. - Whakapapa: Understand data origins, relationships, and context. - Whanaungatanga: Maintain relationships and accountability. - Kotahitanga: Support collective Māori benefit. - Manaakitanga: Ensure respectful and reciprocal data practices. - Kaitiakitanga: Provide responsible guardianship and governance.

Discussion: Can an AI research project comply with the Privacy Act but still fail to respect Māori Data Sovereignty?

Takeaway: Legal privacy compliance and Māori Data Sovereignty are complementary but distinct responsibilities.

Data Governance in the Public Sector

  • Data governance = Policies, roles, and processes for managing data responsibly and effectively throughout its lifecycle (Wang et al. 2024).

  • Key mechanisms in New Zealand:

    • Government Chief Data Steward — leadership and standards.
    • Algorithm Charter — transparency, fairness, and accountability.
    • Agency-level governance — data stewardship, access controls, and oversight.

From principles to practice: Making Data, Algorithms, and Platforms trustworthy.

Key message: Governance translates privacy, Māori Data Sovereignty, and ethical considerations into operational decisions.

Examples: - Chief Data Steward: Stats NZ provides government-wide data leadership and promotes common standards. - Algorithm Charter: An agency developing an AI model should assess bias, explain its purpose, and ensure appropriate human oversight. - Agency governance: Researchers accessing IDI data must meet the Five Safes requirements, including approved projects, people, and secure access.

Māori Data Sovereignty: Governance should enable meaningful Māori authority and participation in decisions about Māori data.

Takeaway: Governance defines who makes decisions, under what rules, and who is accountable.

Data: Research Data Landscape in New Zealand

Key message: Data is the first practical component of public sector Data Science, alongside Algorithm and Platform.

  • Governance determines how data can be accessed, managed, and used.
  • NZ offers both open datasets and restricted research data.
  • The IDI demonstrates how large-scale data integration operates within a governed environment.

Transition: We begin with the available research data sources before focusing on the IDI.

Research Data Sources in Aotearoa NZ

  • Open and publicly accessible data:
    • data.govt.nz — Government open data portal.
    • Aotearoa Data Explorer — Official population, social, and economic statistics.
    • Environmental Data — Climate, water, and environmental information.
    • Health Data — Health statistics and publications.
    • Education Counts — Education statistics and indicators.
  • Restricted research data: Stats NZ’s Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI) and Longitudinal Business Database (LBD).

Key message: NZ provides both open data and controlled-access microdata.

Examples: - Open data: Download regional population and health statistics to visualise diabetes prevalence or socioeconomic inequalities. - Restricted data: Study individual-level relationships between income, education, and health outcomes using linked IDI records.

Distinction: Open data supports accessible analysis; restricted microdata enables more detailed research but requires approval and safeguards.

Transition: Our main focus is the IDI, one of NZ’s most important research data infrastructures.

What are the IDI and LBD?

  • IDI → Linked data about people and households.
  • LBD → Linked data about businesses and economic activity.

Key message: IDI and LBD enable researchers to analyse linked information across time and domains.

Examples: - IDI: Link health, education, income, and housing records to study factors associated with diabetes. - LBD: Analyse how business characteristics, innovation, and employment change over time.

Important: De-identified data is not necessarily anonymous. Researchers must follow confidentiality and access requirements.

Transition: The real value comes from linking information that would otherwise remain in separate datasets.

Why the IDI Matters

  • Integrated: Connects information across health, education, employment, housing, and other domains.
  • Longitudinal: Tracks outcomes and changes over time.
  • Population-scale: Supports research across large segments of New Zealand’s population.
  • Policy-relevant: Enables cross-sector research and programme evaluation (Milne et al. 2019; Milne 2022).

Key strength: Understanding complex social outcomes through linked, longitudinal data.

Key message: IDI allows researchers to study relationships across sectors and over time.

Examples: - Health: Investigate how income, housing, and education relate to future diabetes risk. - Education: Examine transitions from secondary education to tertiary study and employment. - Policy: Evaluate whether participation in a government programme is associated with improved long-term outcomes.

Data Science relevance: Supports predictive modelling, longitudinal analysis, and causal research designs, although data linkage alone does not establish causality.

Caution: The IDI is not a complete census of every activity or person; coverage varies by dataset.

IDI Governance: The Five Safes

Access to the IDI is governed by Stats NZ’s Five Safes framework (Statistics New Zealand 2025).

Principle Key Requirement
Safe People Approved and appropriately trained researchers
Safe Projects Approved research with public benefit
Safe Data Data prepared to minimise identification risks
Safe Settings Secure, controlled research environments
Safe Outputs Confidentiality-checked research results

The Five Safes balance research utility with privacy and confidentiality protection.

Key message: The Five Safes operationalise governance throughout the research lifecycle.

Example — Diabetes research using IDI: - People: Researchers complete training and obtain approval. - Projects: The diabetes research proposal is assessed for public benefit and appropriate use. - Data: Researchers access de-identified, linked health and socioeconomic records. - Settings: Analysis takes place in an approved secure environment. - Outputs: Tables, figures, and model results undergo confidentiality checks before release.

Important: No single safeguard is sufficient. Protection comes from combining all five.

Connection: This is a practical application of the governance concepts introduced in Module 1.

Limitations of the IDI

  • Coverage & Quality: Missing information, linkage errors, and administrative data limitations.
  • Timeliness: Updates and reporting lags vary across datasets.
  • Access & Security: Research approvals, secure environments, and output checks.
  • Computing: Software, infrastructure, and resource constraints.
  • Equity & Governance: Māori Data Sovereignty and representational concerns.

Key challenge: Balancing data utility, accessibility, privacy, and equity.

Key message: The IDI is powerful, but researchers must understand its limitations.

Examples: - Coverage: Hospital data captures many serious conditions, but detailed primary care consultations and clinical notes are generally unavailable. - Quality: Administrative records may contain missing values, inconsistent coding, and linkage errors that affect AI predictions. - Timeliness: Some datasets are updated later than others, limiting real-time analysis. - Access: Project approval and output checking can slow research and collaboration. - Computing: Large-scale machine learning may be constrained by available computing resources and approved software. - Equity: Incomplete or uneven representation can affect Māori and other population groups. Māori data governance also requires attention beyond statistical representativeness.

Discussion: How can we improve research accessibility and AI innovation without compromising privacy or Māori Data Sovereignty?

Takeaway: The IDI is valuable national research infrastructure, but its limitations must be considered when designing research and interpreting results.

Part 2: Algorithm & Platform

Algorithm: Synthetic Data, XAI & AI Adoption

Key message: Algorithms turn governed data into insights, predictions, and services.

Three topics: - Synthetic Data: Improve data availability and support privacy-conscious development. - XAI: Make model behaviour more understandable. - AI Adoption: Translate technical capabilities into responsible public sector practice.

Connection: Data enables algorithms; governance determines how they can be responsibly developed and deployed.

Synthetic Data: Definition & Motivation

Synthetic data = Artificially generated data that mimics patterns and relationships in real datasets (Raghunathan 2021).

Why generate it? - Quantity: Limited observations and rare events. - Quality: Missing values and imbalanced datasets. - Availability: Restricted access to sensitive data.

Key message: Synthetic data can help overcome limitations in real-world datasets.

Examples: - Quantity: Rare disease datasets may have too few observations for robust model development. - Quality: Synthetic generation may help explore class imbalance, but cannot automatically fix source-data bias. - Availability: Researchers could use an approved synthetic IDI-like dataset to develop SQL queries and test analytical pipelines outside secure environments.

Important: Synthetic data is not automatically anonymous or privacy-preserving. Generative models may memorise or disclose sensitive information.

Synthetic Data: Methods & Evolution

  • Statistical methods → Sampling, probabilistic models, copulas.
  • Deep generative models → VAEs and GANs.
  • Recent approaches → Diffusion models and LLMs.

Key message: Tabular Data Synthesis (TDS) uses increasingly sophisticated methods to capture relationships between variables.

Examples: - Statistical: Copulas model dependencies among age, income, and health outcomes. - VAEs/GANs: Learn complex nonlinear relationships in mixed-type data. - Diffusion: Generate synthetic records through iterative denoising. - LLMs: Generate structured records using prompts or learned patterns.

Caution: These methods overlap historically. Newer approaches are not necessarily better for every dataset.

Transition: Regardless of the generation method, synthetic data must be evaluated before use.

Synthetic Data: Value & Evaluation

Public sector applications: - Augment → Support development with limited data. - Balance → Explore under-represented groups. - Enable Access → Develop and test without directly sharing real records.

Four evaluation dimensions:

Dimension Key Question
Fidelity Does it resemble real data?
Utility Is it useful for analysis and ML?
Privacy Could real individuals be exposed?
Fairness Are biases reproduced or amplified?

Key message: Synthetic data offers opportunities, but quality and privacy require separate evaluation.

Examples: - Fidelity: Compare distributions, correlations, and relationships between real and synthetic health records. - Utility: Train a diabetes prediction model on synthetic data and evaluate it on held-out real data (TSTR). - Privacy: Test membership inference, attribute inference, and record linkage risks. - Fairness: Compare model performance across Māori, Pacific, and other population groups.

Public sector example: A synthetic IDI proxy could support code development and training outside secure labs, subject to disclosure-risk assessment and approval.

Important: Synthetic data does not automatically resolve privacy obligations or Māori Data Sovereignty concerns.

Takeaway: High fidelity does not guarantee utility, privacy, or fairness.

XAI: Why Explainability Matters

  • Predictive performance alone is not sufficient for high-impact decisions.
  • Interpretability supports transparency, trust, and human oversight.

Key consideration: Balancing accuracy, interpretability, and accountability.

Key message: Government AI must balance predictive performance with the need to understand and justify decisions.

Example — Health risk prediction: - Logistic regression: Often easier to interpret, with clear model coefficients. - Random forest / XGBoost: Can capture complex relationships but may require post-hoc explanations. - Deep learning: Can model complex patterns but is often harder to interpret directly.

Public sector example: A government agency develops an AI model to identify people at risk of long-term unemployment.

A complex model achieves 86% accuracy, while an interpretable model achieves 84%.

Is the 2% improvement worth the additional complexity, especially if predictions influence access to support services?

Important caveat: The diagram illustrates a common perceived trade-off, not a universal rule. More complex models are not necessarily more accurate.

Transition: XAI methods such as SHAP and LIME help explain complex model predictions.

XAI: Methods & Government Applications

Method Purpose
Linear models / Trees Intrinsically interpretable predictions
SHAP Feature contributions
LIME Local prediction explanations
PDP Average model response to features
Counterfactuals Changes that alter predictions

Applications: Health risk prediction • Tax compliance • Social services

Key message: Different XAI methods explain different aspects of model behaviour.

Examples: - SHAP (Health): Identify variables contributing to an individual’s predicted diabetes risk. - LIME (Tax): Explain why an anomaly detection model flagged a transaction for further review. - PDP (Social services): Explore how predicted outcomes vary with income or employment history. - Counterfactuals: Identify feasible changes that would alter a model’s prediction.

Limitations: - SHAP contributions are not causal effects. - LIME explanations can vary with sampling. - PDP can be misleading with correlated variables. - Counterfactual changes may be unrealistic or inappropriate.

Government relevance: XAI supports transparency, scrutiny, and human oversight, but must be combined with model validation and governance.

AI Adoption in Government: Opportunities

AI can improve public services through:

  • Productivity → Automate routine administrative tasks.
  • Decision Support → Assist evidence-based decisions.
  • Service Delivery → Improve responsiveness and accessibility.
  • Policy Insights → Analyse complex population and economic trends.

Goal: Better public outcomes, not simply more automation.

Key message: AI adoption should be driven by public value and clearly defined problems.

Examples: - Productivity: Summarise lengthy policy documents or assist with drafting routine reports, with human review. - Decision Support: Predict hospital demand to support workforce and resource planning. - Service Delivery: Help people navigate government information and services through an AI assistant. - Policy Insights: Analyse employment, education, and health patterns to support policy development.

Discussion: Which tasks should AI automate, and which should remain under human decision-making?

Important: AI-generated outputs require appropriate verification, especially in high-impact settings.

AI Adoption in Government: Challenges

  • Data → Quality, availability, privacy, and sovereignty.
  • Algorithms → Bias, explainability, reliability, and safety.
  • Platforms → Security, infrastructure, and integration.
  • People & Governance → Skills, accountability, trust, and oversight.

Key challenge: Moving from promising AI pilots to safe, sustainable public services.

Key message: AI adoption is an organisational and governance challenge, not only a technical one.

Examples: - Data: Sensitive government records cannot simply be uploaded into an external AI service. - Algorithms: An eligibility prediction model may reproduce historical inequalities. - Platforms: An AI prototype may work in a notebook but lack secure deployment, monitoring, and integration. - People: Staff need training to recognise hallucinations, limitations, and inappropriate reliance on AI.

NZ context: Consider the Privacy Act, Algorithm Charter commitments where applicable, Māori Data Sovereignty, and agency-specific security and assurance requirements.

Takeaway: Successful AI adoption requires coordinated development across Data, Algorithm, Platform, and Governance.

From Experimentation to Responsible AI Adoption

A practical pathway:

  1. Identify → Define the public problem and expected benefit.
  2. Assess → Evaluate data readiness, risks, and governance.
  3. Pilot → Develop and validate with appropriate users.
  4. Deploy → Implement secure systems with human oversight.
  5. Monitor → Evaluate performance, fairness, and outcomes.

Responsible AI = Public Value + Technical Assurance + Governance

Key message: AI adoption is a lifecycle, not a one-off technology purchase.

Example — Government AI assistant: 1. Identify: Reduce time spent searching policy guidance. 2. Assess: Classify information and determine which documents can be used. 3. Pilot: Test answer accuracy, hallucinations, and user experience. 4. Deploy: Apply access controls, logging, and human escalation. 5. Monitor: Track errors, usage, user feedback, and changes to source documents.

Final takeaway: Synthetic data helps address data access challenges; XAI supports model understanding; governance and platforms enable responsible deployment.

Transition: Next, we examine the Platform component — the infrastructure required to develop and operate AI securely.

Platform: Tools & Infrastructure in Government

Key message: Platforms provide the infrastructure needed to develop, deploy, and maintain Data Science solutions.

  • Data: Storage, processing, and access.
  • Algorithm: Model development, testing, and deployment.
  • Governance: Security, approvals, auditability, and oversight.

Example: A government AI model requires more than Python code — it needs secure data access, reproducible development, deployment infrastructure, and monitoring.

Transition: We will briefly review the tools commonly used across this lifecycle.

Development, Collaboration & Automation

  • Programming: R, Python, and SQL for analysis, modelling, and data processing.
  • Version Control: GitHub, GitLab, Azure Repos for collaboration and audit trails.
  • CI/CD: GitHub Actions and Azure DevOps for automated testing and deployment.
  • Development Environments: Posit, VS Code, and Jupyter for reproducible workflows.

Goal: Reproducible, collaborative, and maintainable Data Science.

Key message: Government Data Science requires reproducible and auditable development.

Examples: - R/Python: Develop a diabetes risk prediction model. - SQL: Extract and transform data from a warehouse. - Git: Track changes to model code and enable peer review. - CI/CD: Automatically test code and deploy approved updates. - Posit/Jupyter: Provide collaborative environments for analysis.

Government context: Version control and deployment approvals support accountability and traceability.

Important: Sensitive data, credentials, and confidential outputs should not be committed to source-code repositories.

Data Warehousing & Compute Platforms

  • Cloud warehouses / lakehouses: Snowflake, Databricks, Microsoft Fabric, AWS.
  • Secure research environments: Stats NZ Data Labs and other approved environments.
  • Cloud / Hybrid Infrastructure: Balance scalability, cost, security, and data sovereignty.

Goal: Secure, scalable, and governed data processing.

Key message: Data platforms provide the storage and computing infrastructure for analytics and AI.

Examples: - Snowflake: Centralise administrative datasets and support SQL-based analytics. - Databricks: Process large datasets and develop machine learning pipelines. - Microsoft Fabric: Integrate data engineering, analytics, and reporting. - AWS: Provide cloud infrastructure for data storage, computing, and AI services. - Stats NZ Data Labs: Enable approved research using restricted linked data.

Government considerations: Data classification, access control, cost, security, and Māori Data Sovereignty should inform platform decisions.

Important: Cloud hosting alone does not guarantee privacy compliance or data sovereignty.

Analytics, Applications & Reporting

  • Analysis & AI: R, Python, SQL.
  • Interactive Applications: Shiny (R), Dash (Python).
  • Enterprise Reporting: Power BI.
  • Collaboration & Publishing: Posit tools and approved deployment services.

Goal: Turn analysis into accessible, actionable insights.

Key message: Data Science creates value when analytical results are accessible to appropriate users.

Examples: - Power BI: An executive dashboard monitors employment trends and programme outcomes. - Shiny: An interactive application allows analysts to explore health inequalities across regions. - Dash: A Python-based application presents model predictions and explanations. - Posit: Supports developing and publishing R/Python applications in approved environments.

Government context: Dashboards and applications require appropriate access controls, quality assurance, and ongoing maintenance.

Important: Aggregated dashboards can still present disclosure risks, particularly for small population groups.

Putting It All Together

A typical government Data Science workflow:

Data → Development → Modelling → Deployment → Decision Support

  • Data: Snowflake, Databricks, secure research environments.
  • Development: SQL, R/Python, Git, CI/CD.
  • Modelling: Predictive AI, synthetic data, XAI.
  • Delivery: Power BI, Shiny, Dash, APIs.

Governance applies throughout — from data access to deployment and monitoring.

Key message: Platforms connect data, algorithms, and decision-makers within a governed workflow.

Example — Government health analytics: 1. Data: Access approved health and socioeconomic records. 2. Development: Use SQL and Python/R to prepare data and build models. 3. Modelling: Develop a diabetes risk model and use XAI to examine predictions. 4. Deployment: Publish approved aggregate insights through Power BI or Shiny. 5. Monitoring: Review data quality, model performance, access, and security.

Important: Restricted IDI microdata and derived outputs must remain within approved environments unless authorised for release. A dashboard must not directly expose unapproved IDI information.

Takeaway: There is no universal technology stack. Platform selection depends on security, governance, skills, cost, and existing agency infrastructure.

Workshop conclusion: Governance establishes accountability, Data provides evidence, Algorithms create analytical capabilities, and Platforms enable responsible delivery.

Group Project: Dementia Research Plan

Design a Responsible Data Science Project

In groups of 4–5 (10 min): Develop a one-page dementia research plan using the four components.

Component Key Questions
Governance What approvals, ethical considerations, and Māori data governance are needed?
Data Which datasets? How will dementia be identified and data quality assessed?
Algorithm Prediction or causal analysis? Which methods?
Platform Which tools and secure environments? How will results be released?

Group sharing (10 min): Each group presents its proposed approach or biggest challenge (≤2 min).

Purpose: Apply Governance, Data, Algorithm, and Platform to a realistic NZ public sector research problem.

Example — Predicting dementia risk: - Governance: Consider ethics approval, Stats NZ access requirements, and meaningful Māori involvement from project design. - Data: Link hospital, pharmaceutical, demographic, and socioeconomic records in the IDI. Consider incomplete dementia identification and missing primary care diagnoses. - Algorithm: Develop a risk prediction model; use XAI to examine important predictors. Consider synthetic data for development, subject to privacy evaluation. - Platform: Analyse restricted data in an approved secure environment. Release only confidentiality-checked outputs.

Facilitation: - Encourage groups to define a clear research question before choosing methods. - Ask whether their data and methods can actually answer that question. - Look for connections between governance requirements and technical decisions. - There is no single correct design; prioritise justification and feasibility.

Discussion prompt: What is the biggest barrier to delivering your proposed project responsibly?
